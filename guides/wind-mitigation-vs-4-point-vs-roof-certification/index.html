Wind mitigation vs. 4-point vs. roof certification inspections in Florida

Florida insurers ask for three different short inspections, and they answer three different questions. A wind mitigation form can lower your hurricane-wind premium. A 4-point inspection and a roof inspection help an insurer decide whether it will write or renew an older home at all. This guide explains each one from official Florida sources, then shows what a handful of local inspectors publish as prices.

Sources checked Nov 20, 2026 (America/New_York) Official sources: Florida Statutes, DFS, OIR, FL Admin. Code, Citizens No referral fees

Short version. If your insurer or agent asked for a specific form, get that form. If you want wind-premium discounts, you need the Uniform Mitigation Verification Inspection Form, OIR-B1-1802; since April 1, 2026 inspections must use the Rev. 04/26 version (DFS). A 4-point or roof report does not verify mitigation features — Citizens’ own roof form says so and points you to OIR-B1-1802 (Citizens). Rules for 4-point and roof reports are set by each insurer, so confirm with yours before you book.

Report 1

Wind mitigation: the Uniform Mitigation Verification Inspection Form

What it is

Florida law requires the Financial Services Commission to adopt, by rule, “a uniform mitigation verification inspection form that shall be used by all insurers when submitted by policyholders for the purpose of factoring discounts for wind insurance” (s. 627.711(2)(a), Fla. Stat.). That form is OIR-B1-1802, adopted by Rule 69O-170.0155, F.A.C. According to the Florida Department of Financial Services, all admitted insurers have been required since July 1, 2008 to accept a valid, properly signed form.

The current form asks the inspector to record, with supporting proof, the building code in force when the home was permitted, the design-wind-speed region, roof slope, roof covering and product approvals, roof deck attachment, roof-to-wall attachment, roof geometry, sealed roof deck / secondary water resistance, and opening protection (OIR-B1-1802 Rev. 04/26).

Who may sign it

Under s. 627.711(2)(a), an insurer must accept a form signed by any of these “authorized mitigation inspectors”:

  1. A home inspector licensed under s. 468.8314 who has completed at least 3 hours of hurricane mitigation training approved by the Construction Industry Licensing Board — covering mitigation techniques and the form — and a proficiency exam;
  2. A building code inspector certified under s. 468.607;
  3. A general, building, or residential contractor licensed under s. 489.111;
  4. A professional engineer licensed under s. 471.015;
  5. A professional architect licensed under s. 481.213; or
  6. Any other individual or entity the insurer recognizes as qualified to complete the form.

DFS adds that licensed home inspectors must then complete at least 2 hours of continuing education each year related to mitigation inspection and the form. The signer must inspect the home personally; only engineers (s. 471.015) and contractors (s. 489.111) may send a direct employee — not an independent contractor — and they are liable for that employee’s work (s. 627.711(3) and (5)). The statute also bars inspectors from paying insurance agents for referrals (s. 627.711(6)).

Our county directories list home-inspector licenses from the DBPR extract. The public extract does not show whether a home inspector has completed the hurricane-mitigation training, so a license on its own is not proof the person may sign OIR-B1-1802. Ask the inspector.

The April 1, 2026 form change

What we could verify from official sources:

  • A new revision is mandatory. DFS says OIR approved OIR-B1-1802 (Rev. 04/26) and it “must be used for all inspections as of April 1, 2026.” Inspections before that date may use the older Rev. 01/12 form, which took effect February 1, 2012 (DFS).
  • It was adopted by rule. The Florida Administrative Code lists Rule 69O-170.0155 (Forms) with an effective date of 4/1/2026; the proposed-rule notice (Oct. 15, 2025) described the change as implementing s. 627.0629(1) and incorporating an updated OIR-B1-1802 (flrules.org).
  • It is longer and more detailed. DFS describes the new form as “more comprehensive with additional categories” and says it “specifies the documentation that must be used to verify entries on the form” (DFS). Comparing the two official PDFs: the 2012 form is 4 pages with 7 numbered questions; the 2026 form is 6 pages with 9, adding a Region question (design wind speed per ASCE 7-22) and a Roof Slope question, and expanding secondary water resistance to “Sealed Roof Deck/Secondary Water Resistance.” The 2026 form requires at least one photograph or document as proof for each attribute marked in questions 2 through 9 (Rev. 04/26, Rev. 01/12).
  • The signer list did not change. Both versions list the same license types that s. 627.711 allows.

If you have a valid form completed before April 1, 2026, DFS says a completed form is valid for up to five years absent material changes or inaccuracies. Whether your insurer will ask for a new one is up to the insurer; ask your agent.

How wind mitigation credits lower premiums

  • Florida law requires residential property rate filings to include “actuarially reasonable discounts, credits, or other rate differentials, or appropriate reductions in deductibles” for homes with features shown to reduce windstorm loss — including wind uplift prevention, roof strength, roof covering performance, roof-to-wall strength, wall-to-floor-to-foundation strength, opening protection, and window, door, and skylight strength (s. 627.0629(1), Fla. Stat.).
  • OIR determines the discounts that reflect the full actuarial value of those features and must re-evaluate them on or before January 1, 2025 and every 5 years after. Since October 1, 2023, each insurer must describe its hurricane mitigation discounts on its website, reachable from its home page or its main property-insurance page (s. 627.0629(1)).
  • Rate filings split the premium into a hurricane portion and an all-other-perils portion, and premium notices show both (s. 627.0629(4)). Mitigation discounts apply only to the hurricane-wind portion, and they are not added together: “when one discount is applied, other discounts are reduced until you reach your maximum discount” (OIR-B1-1655, Notice of Premium Discounts for Hurricane Loss Mitigation).
  • Insurers must send that notice, with their discount ranges, with every new policy and at each renewal (s. 627.711(1); DFS). Your own notice is the best place to see what your carrier offers.
  • An insurer may, at its own expense, have a submitted form independently verified before accepting it (DFS).

Report 2

The 4-point inspection

A 4-point inspection is not a state form and not a full home inspection. It is a condition report on the roof, electrical, HVAC, and plumbing systems that an insurer uses for underwriting. There is no statute that fixes the form or the signer list, so each insurer sets its own requirements. The clearest public example is Citizens Property Insurance Corporation:

  • The 4-Point Inspection Form on Citizens’ website (form Insp4pt 09 13) says: “A 4-point inspection is required for all homeowner, dwelling and mobile home applications for properties more than 30 years old.” It says the specific form is not required, but any other inspection “must include at least this level of detail,” and photos must accompany the form (Citizens 4-Point form).
  • Who may sign (Citizens): the form must be completed, signed, and dated by a verifiable Florida-licensed professional. Citizens’ examples: a general, residential, or building contractor; a building code inspector; a registered architect; a home inspector; a professional engineer; or a building code official authorized by the state to verify code compliance. Trade-specific licensees may sign only their own section — e.g., a roofing inspector may sign only the roof section (Citizens 4-Point form).
  • Aluminum wiring remediation must be documented separately and certified by a licensed electrician (Citizens 4-Point form).
  • Citizens says its 4-point form may be accepted in place of its Roof Condition Certification Form if at least two photos of the roof are provided (Citizens 4-Point form).

Other insurers can set different age thresholds, forms, and signer rules. Ask your agent which form they accept.

Report 3

Roof inspection / roof certification

“Roof certification” usually means a roof condition report: covering type, age, remaining useful life, permit and update dates, and visible damage or leaks, with photos. Like the 4-point, the form is set by the insurer. Two official sources define who can do it:

  • Florida law (homeowners’ policies issued or renewed on or after July 1, 2022): an insurer may not refuse to issue or renew a homeowner’s policy solely because a roof is less than 15 years old. For a roof at least 15 years old, the insurer must let the homeowner get a roof inspection by an “authorized inspector,” at the homeowner’s expense, before requiring replacement, and it may not refuse to issue or renew solely because of roof age if that inspection shows 5 or more years of useful life left (s. 627.7011(5), Fla. Stat.).
  • Who counts as an “authorized inspector” under that law: an inspector approved by the insurer who is a licensed home inspector (s. 468.8314); a certified building code inspector (s. 468.607); a general, building, or residential contractor (s. 489.111) or a roofing contractor; a professional engineer (s. 471.015); a professional architect (s. 481.213); or another individual or entity the insurer recognizes as qualified (s. 627.7011(5)(a)). That subsection does not apply to mobile home policies (s. 627.7011(6)(b)).
  • Citizens: its sample Roof Inspection Form (RCF-1 01 18) must be signed by a general, residential, building, or roofing contractor; a building code inspector; or a Florida-licensed home inspector, with photos of each roof slope. The form states: “This form does not verify loss mitigation features. Use Uniform Mitigation Verification Inspection Form OIR-B1-1802.” (Citizens Roof Inspection Form)
