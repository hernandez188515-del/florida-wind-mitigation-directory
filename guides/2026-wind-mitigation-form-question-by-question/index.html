Florida’s 2026 wind mitigation form, question by question

Since April 1, 2026, every Florida wind mitigation inspection has to be written on the revised Uniform Mitigation Verification Inspection Form, OIR-B1-1802 (Rev. 04/26). It is six pages and nine questions. This guide walks through each question in plain English, notes what is new compared with the 2012 form, and lists the paperwork that helps an inspector check the stronger boxes. Everything here comes from the official form and Florida agency pages; we link each one.

Sources checked Nov 23, 2026 (America/New_York) Official sources: OIR forms, DFS, FL Admin. Code, Fla. Stat. No referral fees

Short version. The 2026 form asks more questions (9 instead of 7) and asks for more proof: “At least one photograph or document providing proof must accompany this form to validate each attribute marked in questions 2 through 9” (OIR-B1-1802 Rev. 04/26, page 1). If you have permits, product-approval numbers, shutter or window paperwork, or a FORTIFIED Home certificate, give copies to your inspector — the form says documentation used to validate a feature “must accompany this form.” Discounts apply only to the hurricane-wind part of your premium and are not added together (OIR-B1-1655). How much your insurer credits each answer is set in its filed rates; ask your agent.

Before the questions

The rules printed on page 1

  • Proof for every attribute. The 2012 form required at least one photograph for each attribute in questions 3 through 7. The 2026 form requires at least one photograph or document for each attribute in questions 2 through 9 (Rev. 04/26; Rev. 01/12).
  • FORTIFIED Home certificates now count. A FORTIFIED Home Roof, Silver, or Gold certificate “may be used to validate the attributes marked in questions 4 through 6, and questions 8 through 9”; the inspector checks the certificate type and attaches a photocopy. The 2012 form did not mention FORTIFIED (Rev. 04/26). FORTIFIED is a program of the Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety (fortifiedhome.org).
  • Insurers can ask more. “The insurer may ask additional questions regarding the mitigated feature(s) verified on this form” (Rev. 04/26). DFS adds that an insurer may, at its own expense, have a form independently verified before accepting it (DFS).
  • Valid up to five years. The footer of every page says the form “is valid for up to five (5) years provided no material changes have been made to the structure or inaccuracies found on the form. Some items listed may not qualify for a discount.” The last sentence is new in 2026 (Rev. 04/26).

Question by question

What each question asks

1. Building Code

Which code was in force when the home’s original permit was applied for. The 2026 form has four answers (Rev. 04/26):

  • A. Florida Building Code (FBC) 2001 & 2004. Homes built in 2002/2003 need a permit application dated after 3/1/2002.
  • B. FBC 2007 and later. Homes built in 2007/2008 need a permit application dated after 12/8/2006. This split between 2001/2004 and 2007+ is new; the 2012 form had a single “built in compliance with the FBC” answer.
  • C. High-Velocity Hurricane Zone (Miami-Dade and Broward) only: South Florida Building Code (SFBC-94). Homes built in 1994–1996 need a permit application dated after 9/1/1994.
  • D. Unknown, or does not meet A, B, or C.

Have ready: the original building permit application date if your home was built in one of the boundary years above. The insurer notice says homes built under the 2001 FBC or later (or the 1994 SFBC in Miami-Dade and Broward) “are eligible for a minimum 68% discount on the hurricane-wind portion” of the premium (OIR-B1-1655, Rev. 02/10). That notice is a 2010 template; your insurer’s actual credits are in its own notice.

2. Region New

Location based on design wind speed, using ASCE 7-22 (700-year MRI), Risk Category 2, with a pointer to ascehazardtool.org. Choices: HVHZ; Region 1 (≥ 140 mph); Region 2 (130–139 mph); Region 3 (< 130 mph) (Rev. 04/26). This is a fact about your address, not something you can change.

3. Roof Slope New

Whether the roof is at least 6:12 or less than 6:12. For single-family homes with several slopes, the inspector uses the slope that covers at least two-thirds of the main roof area (Rev. 04/26).

4. Roof Covering

Every roof covering in use (asphalt/fiberglass shingle, concrete/clay tile, synthetic/composite tile, metal, built-up, membrane, other). For each one the inspector records the permit application date or an FBC or Miami-Dade product-approval number and the year it was installed or replaced — or marks that no information was available. Synthetic/composite tile is listed separately on the 2026 form (Rev. 04/26).

Section 4.2 then asks whether all coverings meet the FBC with a product approval current at installation, or have a roofing permit applied for on or after 3/1/2002, or are original on a home built in 2004 or later (answer A); or meet the Miami-Dade / HVHZ test (answer B); or whether one or more (C) or none (D) do.

Have ready: your re-roof permit, or the product-approval number from the roofer’s paperwork. You can look up approvals at the state Florida Building Code product approval search or Miami-Dade product control search.

5. Roof Deck Attachment

The weakest way the roof sheathing is fastened to the trusses or rafters, usually checked in the attic (Rev. 04/26):

  • A. 7/16″ (minimum) plywood/OSB with staples or 6d nails at 6″ edge / 12″ field; batten decking under wood shakes; or any system with at least 55 psf mean ultimate uplift resistance. If nailing or spacing is wider, or decking thinner, the inspector must choose F (Other).
  • B. 7/16″ plywood/OSB with 8d common nails at 12″ max in the field, or equivalent / at least 103 psf.
  • C. 7/16″ plywood/OSB with 8d common nails at 6″ max in the field; dimensional lumber / tongue-and-groove with the required nails per board; or equivalent / at least 182 psf.
  • D. Reinforced concrete roof deck.
  • E. Spray foam with an uplift resistance of 110 psf (FOS 1.5), installed along rafter-deck intersections and all panel joints. New in 2026.
  • F–H. Other; unknown or unidentified; no attic access.

The 2012 form’s answer A had no minimum sheathing thickness and no 55 psf floor, and the 2012 form had no spray-foam answer (Rev. 01/12). If your attic hatch is blocked, the inspector may have to check “No attic access,” so clear it before the visit.

6. Roof to Wall Attachment

The weakest connection between the roof framing and the walls, ignoring hip/valley jacks within five feet of a roof corner. Answers run from A (toenails) through B (clips), C (single wraps), D (double wraps), and E (structural: anchor bolts or reinforced concrete roof), plus F Other, G Unknown, and H No attic access (Rev. 04/26).

What is new in 2026:

  • Each of A–D now also allows a purpose-made connector or structural fastener with a documented wind allowable uplift capacity: 185 lbs or more (A), 386 lbs (B), 535 lbs (C), or 891 lbs (D). B–D require site-specific documentation and installation per the manufacturer’s specifications.
  • The “minimal conditions” for B–D add a single-strap-over-the-truss option and say all retrofit connectors must meet the purpose-made option.
  • Metal connectors “not installed as intended” fall to A, and there is a new answer I. Connection(s) not installed as intended.

Have ready: if you retrofitted hurricane straps, the contractor’s permit and the connector’s product sheet or engineering letter.

7. Roof Geometry

A. Hip roof — no other roof shapes greater than 10% of the total roof-system perimeter (the inspector records both lengths). B. Flat roof — only for buildings with five or more units where at least 90% of the main roof is under 2:12. C. Other — everything else. Porch or carport roofs attached only to the fascia or wall over unenclosed space are not counted. These definitions match the 2012 form (Rev. 04/26; Rev. 01/12).

8. Sealed Roof Deck / Secondary Water Resistance (SWR)

A backup layer that keeps water out if the roof covering blows off. “Standard underlayment or hot-mopped felts do not qualify.” The 2026 form lists four ways to qualify for answer A (Rev. 04/26):

  • Fully adhered polymer-modified bitumen underlayment meeting ASTM D1970;
  • Tape over the deck seams (at least 3.75″ of ASTM D1970 membrane or AAMA 711 Level 3 tape) with felt or synthetic over it;
  • Two layers of qualifying felt or synthetic underlayment (ASTM D226 Type II, D4869 Type III/IV, or D8257) with no tape;
  • Spray foam along rafter-deck intersections and panel joints (with a box for full underside coverage).

Otherwise: B (no sealed roof deck) or C (unknown). The 2012 form described SWR in one sentence — self-adhering modified-bitumen underlayment or a foam adhesive barrier — and did not list the tape or double-layer methods (Rev. 01/12). SWR is usually installed during a re-roof and is hard to see afterward, so the roofer’s invoice or permit and photos are what an inspector can use.

9. Opening Protection

The weakest wind-borne-debris protection on any opening — windows, doors, sliding glass doors, skylights, garage doors (roof and gable vents are excluded). The inspector fills in a chart for each opening type and then picks one answer based on the weakest glazed opening, with a .1/.2/.3 sub-answer for non-glazed doors (Rev. 04/26):

  • A. Verified cyclic-pressure and 9-lb large-missile protection (4.5 lb for skylights) under Miami-Dade PA 201/202/203, FBC TAS 201/202/203, ASTM E1886/E1996, or SSTD 12; garage doors under ANSI/DASMA 115.
  • B. Verified cyclic-pressure and 4–8-lb large-missile protection.
  • C. Plywood or OSB panels meeting FBC 2007 Table 1609.1.2 on all glazed openings.
  • N. Shutters or products that look like A or B but have no documentation, or coverings that can’t be identified.
  • X. One or more glazed openings with no wind-borne-debris protection.
  • Z. One or more openings damaged and in need of repair or replacement. New in 2026.

The 2026 form adds that all openings “must be in good condition” and that “buildings with openings that need replacement do not qualify for wind mitigation rate differentials, regardless of the presence/absence of other features” (Rev. 04/26). One cracked window or damaged garage door can therefore matter more than it used to.

Have ready: product-approval or Miami-Dade NOA numbers, permits, and invoices for impact windows, doors, shutters, and the garage door. Without documentation, protection that looks impact-rated may be marked N.

Who signs, and what they certify

The inspector lists name, license type and number, company, and phone, and checks one license category: a home inspector licensed under s. 468.8314 who “has completed the statutory number of hours of hurricane mitigation training … and completion of a proficiency exam”; a building code inspector (s. 468.607); a general, building, or residential contractor (s. 489.111); a professional engineer (s. 471.015); a professional architect (s. 481.213); or another person or entity the insurer recognizes (Rev. 04/26; s. 627.711(2)). Only contractors and engineers may send a direct employee; everyone else must inspect personally (DFS).

You, the homeowner, also sign — certifying that the named inspector or employee did the inspection and showed you identification. The form warns that knowingly providing a false or fraudulent form to obtain a discount is a first-degree misdemeanor under s. 627.711(7) (Rev. 04/26).

Our county directories list DBPR home-inspector licenses. The public license file does not show whether an inspector completed the hurricane-mitigation training, so ask before you book. See who may sign in our first guide.

Before the inspector arrives

A short checklist drawn from the form

  1. Original building-permit application date (matters for homes built 1994–1996 in the HVHZ, 2002–2003, and 2007–2008) — Q1.
  2. Re-roof permit, roof product-approval number, and year installed for each roof covering — Q4.
  3. Roofer’s paperwork showing the underlayment or seam-tape method, if you had a sealed roof deck installed — Q8.
  4. Permits, product-approval / NOA numbers, or invoices for impact windows, doors, shutters, and the garage door — Q9.
  5. Paperwork for any retrofit hurricane straps or connectors — Q6.
  6. A FORTIFIED Home Roof, Silver, or Gold certificate, if you have one — Q4–6, Q8–9.
  7. Clear access to the attic hatch, and repair any cracked or damaged openings beforehand — Q5, Q6, Q9.
  8. Your insurance company name and policy number, which go on page 1.

Status

Where the rule stands (checked November 23, 2026)

The Florida Administrative Code shows Rule 69O-170.0155 (Forms) with an effective date of 4/1/2026 and lists OIR-B1-1802 among the forms it references. The rule history also shows a January 30, 2026 notice of rule development to incorporate a separate form, OIR-B1-600 (“Uniform Mitigation Verification Form Report”), a May 19, 2026 public workshop, and a “Notice of Change/Withdrawal/Correction” published September 2, 2026 (flrules.org). The text of that September notice did not display when we checked, so we are not describing its effect. The DFS page still says Rev. 04/26 “must be used for all inspections as of April 1, 2026” (DFS).
